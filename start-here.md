# Hazard vs. risk

<p class="lede">A <strong>hazard</strong> is something an ingredient can do under some condition. A <strong>risk</strong> is the chance it actually does that at the amount, and by the route, you're exposed to. Almost every scary ingredient claim is a hazard presented as a risk.</p>

## Three things decide the risk

**The route.** A substance can be hazardous when you breathe it in as a fine powder and inert when it sits on your skin. Makeup reaches you in different ways: lip products are partly swallowed, loose powders can be inhaled, eye products touch the thin skin and membranes around your eyes, and most foundations and blushes stay on the surface of the skin.

**The dose.** Regulators estimate how much of an ingredient you'd absorb each day from a product and compare it with the highest dose that caused no effect in studies. The EU's Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety (SCCS) wants that gap, the *margin of safety*, to be at least 100-fold.

**Who you are.** Someone with a fragrance allergy faces a real risk from a product that's fine for most people. Allergy is the most common real harm from cosmetics.

## Three examples

| Ingredient | The hazard | Why the risk differs by product |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Titanium dioxide]({{ '/ingredients/titanium-dioxide/' | relative_url }}) | Rats that inhaled large amounts of fine powder developed lung tumours | A loose powder can be inhaled; a cream foundation can't. On lips, a little is swallowed, which raises a different, still-open question |
| Formaldehyde | A known human carcinogen when inhaled | Hair-straightening treatments heat it into the air you breathe. The preservatives that release tiny amounts in a mascara mainly matter for people allergic to them |
| [Fragrance]({{ '/ingredients/fragrance/' | relative_url }}) | A leading cause of cosmetic skin allergy | No issue for most people; a real one for the allergic minority, who can't always tell what's in the blend |

## What an IARC classification means

The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) sorts substances by how strong the evidence is that they *can* cause cancer, not by how likely they are to at everyday levels. Group 2B, "possibly carcinogenic to humans", also includes aloe vera whole-leaf extract and pickled vegetables in the traditional Asian style. A Group 2B listing is a reason to look at the exposure, not a verdict.
