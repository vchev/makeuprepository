## What it is

Titanium dioxide is a bright white mineral pigment used to make products opaque and lighten shades. In lipstick it turns reds into pinks and nudes. Pigment-grade particles are much larger than the nanoparticles used in some sunscreens, although a pigment sample can still contain a share of nano-sized particles.

## Inhalation: real but narrow

IARC classified titanium dioxide as "possibly carcinogenic to humans" (Group 2B) in Volume 93 (2010), based on lung tumours in rats that inhaled high concentrations of fine particles. The evidence in humans was inadequate.

The EU then classified titanium dioxide powder as a suspected carcinogen by inhalation in 2020 (Delegated Regulation 2020/217). EU courts struck that classification down: the General Court annulled it in 2022 and the Court of Justice upheld the annulment on 1 August 2025 (case C-71/23 P) [[1]](#s1).

Separately, the EU's cosmetics rules still keep titanium dioxide powder out of products that could be breathed in. Under [Regulation 2021/850](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2021/850/oj/eng), powder-form TiO2 is allowed only in loose face powders (up to 25%) and hair sprays (up to 1.4%), the limits the SCCS judged safe for those uses.

**What this means for you:** pressed powders, creams, liquids and lipsticks don't put fine powder into your lungs. If you want to be cautious, loose setting powders are the only product type where inhalation is plausible.

## Swallowing: the open question

Lip products are partly swallowed over a day. In 2021 the European Food Safety Authority concluded it could no longer consider titanium dioxide (E171) safe as a food additive because a genotoxicity concern couldn't be ruled out, and the EU removed it from food.

The SCCS looked at the same question for cosmetics. In its final opinion of May 2024 ([SCCS/1661/23](https://health.ec.europa.eu/publications/scientific-advice-titanium-dioxide-tio2-casec-numbers-13463-67-7236-675-5-1317-70-0215-280-1-1317-80_en)), it found the data insufficient to rule out genotoxicity for most titanium dioxide grades in products that can be swallowed, such as lip products and toothpaste, and said it could not recommend safe limits for oral use. Two specific nano grades showed no genotoxicity concern.

**What this means for you:** this is a gap in the data, not evidence of harm. The amount swallowed from lipstick is far smaller than from food that contained E171. If you'd rather not wait for the answer, sheer tints, balms and many deep reds skip titanium dioxide; check for CI 77891 on the label.

## Common claims

- **"Titanium dioxide is a carcinogen, so avoid it in everything."** Overstated. The classification is for inhaled powder only, and it has been annulled in the EU.
- **"It's only a concern in powders, so lipstick is fine."** Mostly right, but it skips the open swallowing question for lip products.
