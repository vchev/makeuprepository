## What "fragrance" on a label means

"Fragrance" or "parfum" on a label can stand for a blend of dozens of ingredients. In the US, the FDA says fragrance and flavor ingredients "can be listed simply as 'Fragrance' or 'Flavor'", because labeling law can't be used to force a company to reveal trade secrets [[1]](#s1). The FDA also notes it doesn't have the same authority to require allergen labeling for cosmetics as it has for food.

## What the evidence says

The documented harm from fragrance is **allergic contact dermatitis**: an itchy, red rash where the product touches skin, sometimes appearing days after use. Once you're sensitised to a fragrance ingredient, the allergy usually lasts for life. Fragrance is consistently among the most common causes of cosmetic allergy in patch-test clinics.

The FDA lists fragrances among the main classes of cosmetic allergens and points to the EU's list of named fragrance allergens [[2]](#s2). Many of those, such as linalool, limonene, citral and geraniol, occur naturally in essential oils, which is why "natural fragrance" isn't a safer swap for allergic people.

## Disclosure

Not naming the ingredients in a fragrance is protected as a trade secret; it isn't a sign that a product is hiding something toxic. The real problem with non-disclosure is that people with a known allergy can't avoid their trigger.

- **EU:** named fragrance allergens must appear on the label above set levels, and Regulation (EU) 2023/1545 expands that list.
- **US:** the 2022 Modernization of Cosmetics Regulation Act (MoCRA) requires the FDA to write a fragrance allergen labeling rule. The FDA had not issued one as of early 2026 [[3]](#s3).

## Who should avoid it

- Anyone with a diagnosed fragrance allergy or a positive patch test to a fragrance mix
- People with eczema or easily irritated skin, as a sensible precaution
- Anyone whose eyelids or lips react to a product: fragrance is a common culprit to rule out first

## Common claims

- **"All scented products are toxic because of hidden ingredients."** Overstated. The evidence supports allergy risk, not general toxicity, and non-disclosure is about trade-secret law.
- **"Rating apps that pass products with fragrance are wrong."** It depends on what you need. If you have a fragrance allergy, treat "fragrance" as a hard no, whatever an app's score says.
