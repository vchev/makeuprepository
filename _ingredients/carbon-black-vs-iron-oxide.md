## What they are

**Carbon black** (CI 77266, sold in the US as D&C Black No. 2) is almost pure carbon made by burning oil or gas under controlled conditions. It gives the deepest black. **Black iron oxide** (CI 77499) is a mineral pigment, usually made synthetically, that gives a softer black. Many "clean" brands use iron oxide and many conventional brands use carbon black, which is why the CI number has become a shortcut on social media.

## What the evidence says

IARC classified carbon black as "possibly carcinogenic to humans" (Group 2B) in Volume 93 (2010), mainly because rats that inhaled high doses developed lung tumours. Studies of factory workers who breathe carbon black dust every day did not show a clear cancer link. A mascara or liner applies a small amount of pigment held in wax or film, so the inhalation route behind the classification hardly applies.

The impurity worth controlling in carbon black is a family of combustion by-products called polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons. US and EU rules allow carbon black in cosmetics only when it meets purity specifications that limit them.

Iron oxides are exempt from FDA batch certification, but the FDA caps their impurities at 10 ppm lead, 3 ppm arsenic and 3 ppm mercury, and allows them in eye-area products [[1]](#s1). Synthetic iron oxides are usually purer than mined ones.

## What actually matters for mascara

- **Eye infections** from old, shared or "wet with saliva" tubes. Replace mascara every few months.
- **Allergy** to preservatives or fragrance, which shows up as itchy, puffy lids.
- **PFAS** in some waterproof formulas, which is a more open question than either pigment.

## Common claims

- **"Check for CI 77266 and buy CI 77499 instead."** Accurate as a label-reading tip, misleading as a safety filter. The label tells you which pigment, not which product is safer.
- **"Carbon black is a carcinogen."** It's a Group 2B hazard by inhalation, not evidence that mascara causes cancer.
